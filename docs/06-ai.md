# 06 AI Standards

# 10. AI Standards

---

## Purpose

This section defines exactly what the AI is allowed to do inside Saati, what data it is allowed to see, how its language must be shaped, and what happens when it fails.

Saati uses AI for exactly one job: turning already-computed wellbeing scores into a short, personalised, plain-English summary that a student reads inside their report.

The AI does not decide what a student's scores are. It does not diagnose. It does not counsel. It writes a summary of numbers that have already been calculated by deterministic, auditable code.

This narrow scope is intentional and non-negotiable. Everything below exists to keep the AI inside that boundary.

---

## Why the Scope Is This Narrow

Saati is not a medical, diagnostic, therapy, or crisis-intervention service. That rule from `CLAUDE.md` applies to every part of the platform, and AI is the part most likely to break it by accident.

Large language models are fluent. Fluency is dangerous here, because a model can produce a confident-sounding clinical opinion, a subtly alarming sentence, or an invented statistic without knowing it has done anything wrong. A model has no license, no training, no liability, and no accountability — a human clinician has all four.

The fix is architectural, not just a prompt instruction: the AI is only ever given numbers that a human-reviewed, deterministic scoring engine already produced (see `docs/05-assessment-engine.md`), and its only output is narrative text describing those numbers. It never sees raw question-by-question answers where that can be avoided, never runs a scoring formula, and never makes a judgment call about a student's mental state. If a future feature seems to require the AI to "decide" something clinically meaningful, that is a signal the feature does not belong in Saati, not a signal to loosen this rule.

---

## Scope Boundary — What the AI Is and Is Not

### The AI is allowed to:

- Read the final, already-computed scores (WHO-5 index, PERMA domain scores, Saati Insights scores for sleep, study, focus, and stress).
- Generate supportive, plain-English narrative text that explains what those scores mean in everyday terms.
- Highlight relative strengths and areas the student might want to focus on, based on the score pattern it was given.
- Reference general, well-established wellbeing concepts (e.g., the value of consistent sleep, the link between social connection and wellbeing) in a light, non-clinical way.
- Adapt tone slightly to score severity within the guardrails defined later in this document (e.g., more gently phrased encouragement when scores are low, never alarmed or clinical language).

### The AI is never allowed to:

- Compute, adjust, override, or "sanity check" any score. Scoring is 100% deterministic code, and it runs before the AI is ever invoked. The AI cannot see raw answers and re-derive a different number, and it has no mechanism to write scores back to the report.
- Diagnose or suggest a diagnosis, disorder, or clinical label of any kind (e.g., must never say or imply "depression," "anxiety disorder," "burnout syndrome," "clinically significant").
- Offer medical, psychiatric, or therapeutic advice, including medication, dosage, or treatment suggestions.
- Act as a crisis counselor, safety planner, or provide "in the moment" crisis support (e.g., must never say "talk to me," "I'm here for you right now," or otherwise position itself as a support channel).
- Predict future medical or mental-health outcomes ("this pattern often leads to...").
- Promise outcomes ("this plan will fix your sleep") — Saati offers evidence-informed insight, not guarantees.
- See or use identifying personal information beyond what is strictly needed to personalise tone (see Input Design below).
- Generate or alter the validated-measure content itself (e.g., it must never paraphrase or rewrite the WHO-5 items or their official interpretation bands).

This is a synthesis-only role. Think of the AI as a skilled writer handed a finished spreadsheet, not a analyst handed raw data.

## Input Design — What Data Reaches the Model

### What is sent

The prompt sent to the AI model contains only what is required to write an accurate, personalised summary:

- The computed WHO-5 score and its standard interpretation band (e.g., "below threshold," "moderate," "high" — using the validated instrument's own published bands, not an invented one).
- The computed PERMA domain scores (Positive Emotion, Engagement, Relationships, Meaning, Accomplishment).
- The computed Saati Insights scores for sleep, study, focus, and stress.
- Minimal context needed for tone calibration — for example, whether this is the student's first assessment or a repeat assessment (so the model can say "since your last check-in" appropriately), and coarse demographic context only if it materially improves relevance (e.g., "university student") and has been explicitly approved for inclusion.
- A fixed system prompt (see Prompt Design below) that never varies per request and is version-controlled like code.

### What is never sent

- Name, email address, student ID, or any other direct identifier. The report is assembled and addressed to the student _after_ the AI returns its text — the model itself never needs to know who it is writing for.
- Free-text answers, journal entries, or any open-ended input the student may have provided elsewhere in the product, unless a specific future feature explicitly requires it and has been through a privacy review.
- Prior assessment history beyond the minimal "first vs. repeat" flag described above.
- Device, location, IP address, or any technical/session metadata.

### Why this matters

This is a direct extension of Principle 6 (Privacy by Design) and Principle 8 (Privacy by Default) from `CLAUDE.md` and the engineering constitution. Two independent reasons make PII minimization non-negotiable here specifically:

1. **Third-party exposure.** Whatever is sent to an AI model provider leaves Saati's own infrastructure. The less personal data included, the smaller the surface area if a provider has a breach, logs prompts for abuse monitoring, or is compelled to disclose data.
2. **No benefit.** A student's name or email does not make the wellbeing summary more accurate or more useful. Where a data field does not improve the output, it must not be sent — this is the same test applied to all data collection under Principle 8.

Every new field considered for inclusion in the AI prompt must pass an explicit review: does this materially improve the summary's usefulness, and is there a less identifying way to get the same benefit? If in doubt, leave it out.

## Prompt Design Principles

The system prompt is a controlled, version-controlled artifact — it is reviewed the same way code is reviewed, not edited ad hoc by whoever is closest to a shipping deadline.

### 1. Supportive and encouraging tone

The model must write the way a thoughtful, warm mentor would — never clinical, never cold, never preachy. Every summary should leave a student feeling _understood_, not judged. This mirrors the experience goals in `CLAUDE.md`: students should feel curious, supported, encouraged, and understood — never judged, diagnosed, scared, or labelled.

### 2. Evidence-grounded language

The model may reference general, well-established wellbeing concepts (sleep hygiene, the connection between social relationships and wellbeing, the value of small consistent habits) but must not invent psychological mechanisms, cite fabricated studies, or state statistics it was not given. If the model does not have a number, it should not state one.

### 3. No diagnostic or alarming language

The prompt explicitly instructs the model to avoid clinical vocabulary and alarming framing. A concrete banned/preferred-phrasing table is maintained alongside the prompt (see Guardrails below) and is treated as living documentation, updated whenever a review surfaces a new problematic phrase.

### 4. Validated Measures vs. Saati Insights — always distinguished in the generated text

Per the Scientific Integrity rule in `CLAUDE.md` ("All reports must clearly distinguish Validated Measures from Saati Insights"), the prompt requires the model to:

- Refer to the WHO-5 and PERMA results using language that makes clear they come from established, independently validated research instruments (e.g., "your WHO-5 wellbeing score, a widely used research measure, shows...").
- Refer to sleep, study, focus, and stress results using language that makes clear these are Saati's own proprietary indicators, informed by evidence but not a clinical or independently validated instrument (e.g., "your Saati sleep insight suggests...").
- Never blend the two into a single unlabeled claim (e.g., never "your overall score of X means you are..." without attributing which measure X came from).

This distinction is enforced both in the prompt instructions and checked in QA review (below) — it is treated as a compliance requirement, not a style preference.

### 5. Always contextualized as wellbeing insight, not medical advice

Every generated summary must read as reflective, educational content about wellbeing — never as a professional opinion. The prompt requires the model to frame observations as "this may be worth noticing" rather than "this indicates." The report itself (not the AI) also carries a persistent, non-AI-generated disclaimer stating that Saati is not medical advice — the AI's job is to write in a way that is consistent with that disclaimer, not to reproduce it.

## Guardrails — What the AI Must Never Say

The system prompt contains explicit, enumerated prohibitions. These are treated as hard constraints, verified in QA (below), not just stylistic suggestions:

- **No diagnosis or diagnostic implication.** Never name or imply a mental health condition, disorder, or syndrome.
- **No medication or treatment advice.** Never mention drugs, dosages, therapy modalities, or specific clinical treatments.
- **No crisis intervention or safety promises.** Never say things like "you're not alone, I'm here," "everything will be okay," or anything positioning the AI or Saati as a support system in a crisis. Saati signposts to real help; it does not attempt to provide that help itself.
- **No certainty beyond the data.** Never claim the score "proves," "confirms," or "means" something beyond what the validated instrument's own published interpretation supports.
- **No guilt or shame framing.** Never imply the student has failed, is doing something wrong, or should feel bad about a low score.
- **No exaggerated claims.** Never promise that following a suggestion "will" improve wellbeing — evidence-informed language stays in "may help" / "is associated with" territory.

### Defined behavior for low or concerning scores

When a computed score falls below a conventional concern threshold — for example, a WHO-5 raw score below the published cutoff commonly used to flag low wellbeing — the system does not rely on the AI to "notice" this and improvise a response. Instead:

1. The **deterministic scoring layer**, not the AI, is responsible for detecting that a threshold has been crossed and setting a flag (e.g., `who5_below_threshold: true`) that is passed into the prompt as a fact, not left for the model to infer from the raw number.
2. When that flag is present, the prompt instructs the model to:
   - Keep the tone warm, calm, and non-alarming — never dramatic or urgent in a way that could frighten the student.
   - Avoid any implication that something is "wrong" with the student; frame it as "your responses suggest this might be a good time to lean on extra support."
   - Include a clear, supportive mention that additional support is available, without the AI itself attempting to provide that support.
3. The **report template** (not the AI) then deterministically appends a fixed, non-AI-generated signposting block whenever the flag is set — for example, contact details for the student's university counseling service (where known) and recognized crisis lines (e.g., a national/regional crisis text line or hotline appropriate to the student's locale). This block is identical every time, legally and clinically reviewed, and never left to model generation. The AI is not trusted to produce crisis-line phone numbers or URLs; it is only trusted to write the warm sentence that introduces the (separately rendered) resource block.
4. The platform never claims, in AI-generated text or otherwise, that Saati itself provides counseling, therapy, or crisis support. The language is always "here is where to find support," never "we are here to support you through this."

This split — deterministic detection and fixed resource content, AI-generated tone only — exists specifically so that the single highest-stakes moment in the product (a student seeing a low wellbeing score) never depends on a language model behaving correctly under pressure.

## Model Selection Considerations

Saati does not mandate a single proprietary AI vendor in this document, because vendor availability, pricing, and safety tooling change faster than an engineering constitution should. Instead, any model used for summary generation must be evaluated against the following criteria, in priority order (consistent with the Engineering Decision Hierarchy in `docs/02-engineering-constitution.md`):

1. **Safety and guardrail support.** Does the provider support system-level instructions the model reliably follows, content-moderation or safety classifiers, and low rates of instruction-ignoring ("jailbreak") behavior? A cheaper or faster model that regularly ignores the guardrails above is not acceptable at any price.
2. **Reliability.** Documented uptime, consistent API behavior, and a track record of not silently changing output quality underneath a pinned model version.
3. **Cost per generation.** Because this is a per-report generation (not a live chat), Saati should favor models priced for batch/asynchronous text generation over premium real-time conversational models, where quality is equivalent.
4. **Latency appropriate to a report-generation flow, not a chat flow.** This is not real-time chat — a student is generating a report, typically expecting to receive an email or download shortly after, not an instant response. A model that takes several seconds to a small number of minutes is acceptable; a model tuned only for sub-second chat responsiveness offers no advantage here and should not be chosen for speed alone if it trades away quality or safety.
5. **Output quality and steerability.** Does the model reliably produce warm, on-tone, accurate summaries that respect the Validated Measures / Saati Insights distinction, without requiring an excessively long or fragile prompt to constrain it?

Model choice should be revisited periodically (not locked in permanently), and any change of model or model version must go through the same QA process described below before it ships — a "smarter" model is not automatically a safer one for this use case.

## QA and Evaluation of AI Outputs

AI-generated text is treated as a product surface that requires testing, the same way a scoring formula or a UI component would. It is never shipped on the assumption that "the prompt looks right."

### Before shipping (pre-release)

- **Golden-set prompt testing.** A maintained set of representative score combinations (high WHO-5/high PERMA; low WHO-5/high PERMA; mixed domain scores; below-threshold WHO-5; first-time vs. repeat assessment; etc.) is run through the model, and every output is manually reviewed against the guardrails above before any prompt or model change ships.
- **Policy compliance check.** Each golden-set output is checked line by line for: diagnostic language, medical/treatment advice, crisis-counselor framing, unearned certainty, guilt/shame framing, and correct Validated Measures vs. Saati Insights attribution.
- **Tone check.** Outputs are reviewed for warmth and encouragement, consistent with the experience goals in `CLAUDE.md` (curious, supported, encouraged, understood — never judged, diagnosed, scared, labelled).
- **Hallucination check.** Reviewers confirm the summary only references numbers and facts actually present in the input — no invented statistics, no fabricated study citations, no claims about the student that weren't in the data.

### After shipping (ongoing)

- **Spot-checking in production.** A sample of real generated summaries is periodically reviewed (with PII already stripped from what reaches the model, review can safely happen on de-identified score/output pairs) to catch drift that golden-set testing didn't anticipate.
- **Regression testing on prompt or model changes.** Any change to the system prompt or underlying model version re-runs the full golden set before deployment — this is treated as a required gate, not an optional courtesy step.
- **Incident process.** If a generated summary is found to have violated a guardrail after shipping, it is treated as a bug: the specific failure is added to the golden set (so it is tested for forever after), and the prompt or model configuration is corrected before the next release.

The golden set itself is a living document — every real-world failure discovered becomes a permanent regression test, so the set of guardrails is enforced by evidence and grows more rigorous over time, not just by intention.

## Fallback Behavior — When AI Generation Fails or Times Out

The AI narrative is an enhancement layered on top of the report. It is never a dependency the rest of the report needs to function.

- The WHO-5 score, PERMA domain scores, Saati Insights scores, and the radar chart are all produced by the deterministic scoring engine and are always available regardless of AI availability.
- If the AI call fails, times out, or returns output that fails an automated safety/format check (e.g., detectable banned phrases, empty response, malformed output), the system does not retry indefinitely or block report delivery.
- Instead, the student receives their full report — scores, chart, and standard evidence-informed guidance text — with a pre-written, non-AI, human-reviewed generic summary in place of the personalised narrative. This fallback text is warm, accurate, and fully guardrail-compliant by construction, because it was written and reviewed once by a human rather than generated per request.
- The student is never shown an error message, a blank section, or a "try again later" prompt in place of their report. The report is always complete and usable; only the personalization of the written narrative is what varies.
- This failure is logged for engineering visibility (so degraded AI availability is noticed and fixed), but it is never surfaced to the student as a failure — from the student's point of view, they simply receive a slightly less personalised, still genuinely useful, wellbeing report.

This guarantees the platform's core promise — an evidence-informed wellbeing report — never depends on a third-party AI provider being available, fast, or well-behaved at the moment a student happens to generate a report.

# Acceptance Criteria

This section is complete when:

- The AI's role is defined narrowly as narrative synthesis only, with computation, diagnosis, and crisis counseling explicitly and permanently out of scope.
- The exact data sent to the AI model is documented, and a clear PII-minimization rule governs what may and may not be included in prompts.
- Prompt design principles guarantee a supportive, evidence-grounded, non-diagnostic tone and a clear, consistent distinction between Validated Measures and Saati Insights language.
- Guardrails explicitly list prohibited content and define a concrete, deterministic (not AI-improvised) behavior for low or concerning scores, including signposting to real external support without Saati claiming to provide that support itself.
- Model selection is governed by documented criteria (safety/guardrail support, reliability, cost, latency, quality) rather than a hardcoded vendor mandate.
- A QA process exists for AI outputs, covering both pre-release golden-set testing and ongoing post-release spot-checking, with a defined process for turning discovered failures into permanent regression tests.
- Fallback behavior guarantees the report is always complete and usable even when AI generation fails, with no student-visible error state.

# Common Mistakes to Avoid

- Letting the AI see raw question-by-question answers or compute anything resembling a score.
- Sending names, emails, or other identifiers into the AI prompt "just in case it's useful."
- Allowing the model to use clinical vocabulary ("disorder," "syndrome," "depression") anywhere in generated text.
- Treating the low-WHO-5 signposting response as something the AI should decide or word from scratch each time, rather than a deterministic flag plus a fixed, reviewed resource block.
- Blending Validated Measures and Saati Insights language into a single unattributed claim.
- Choosing a model purely for lowest latency or lowest cost without evaluating its guardrail reliability first.
- Shipping a prompt or model change without re-running the golden test set.
- Letting report delivery block on, retry against, or otherwise depend on the AI call succeeding.
- Showing the student an error, blank state, or "AI unavailable" message instead of the fallback narrative.
- Treating the system prompt as something that can be casually edited without review, the same way production code would not be.

# Future Enhancements

- Formal, versioned prompt-change review process analogous to an Architecture Decision Record (ADR).
- Automated (not just manual) classifier that screens every generated summary for banned phrases and diagnostic language before it reaches a student, in addition to human QA sampling.
- Expanded, locale-aware signposting content (region-specific crisis lines and university resource directories) as Saati expands beyond its initial markets.
- Longitudinal tone consistency checks across repeat assessments, so a returning student's summaries read as part of one coherent, encouraging narrative over time.
- Formal AI governance framework with named human oversight responsibilities, referenced in `docs/02-engineering-constitution.md`'s own Future Enhancements list.
